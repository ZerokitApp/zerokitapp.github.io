How to Price Freelance Work in 2026 (Without Underselling Yourself)

July 2026 · 6 min read

Most freelancers set their first rate by guessing. They look at what a friend charges, pick a round number that feels reasonable, and adjust later if clients push back. The problem is that this approach almost always lands too low, and once a rate is set with a client, raising it later is far harder than starting correctly.

This guide walks through a more reliable way to price your work, whether you charge hourly or per project.

Start with your actual costs, not a guess

Before picking a number, work out what you actually need to earn. This means accounting for:

  • Your target take-home income — what you want to earn after tax and expenses
  • Non-billable time — admin, invoicing, finding clients, and learning are all real hours that don't get billed directly, but still need to be paid for by your rate
  • Business expenses — software subscriptions, equipment, a portion of your workspace, insurance
  • Taxes — freelance income is usually taxed differently than salaried income, and this needs to be priced in, not left as a surprise later

Once these are laid out, a fair hourly rate becomes a calculation rather than a guess.

Hourly vs. project pricing

Both models are common, and the right choice depends on the type of work.

Hourly pricing works well when:

  • The scope is genuinely unclear or likely to change
  • The work is ongoing or open-ended, like a retainer relationship
  • You're new to a type of project and don't yet know how long it reliably takes

Project pricing works well when:

  • The scope is well defined and unlikely to shift significantly
  • You've done similar work before and can estimate time accurately
  • The client wants cost certainty upfront, which many do

Project pricing also tends to reward efficiency: if you get faster at a type of work over time, your effective hourly return goes up, while hourly billing caps your income at your hours worked no matter how efficient you become.

Common pricing mistakes

Pricing to match a client's budget instead of your costs. If a client says their budget is lower than your rate, the answer isn't always to lower your rate; sometimes it's a smaller scope, or it's not the right client.

Forgetting non-billable time entirely. A freelancer working 40 hours a week might only bill 25-30 of those hours. If your rate is calculated as if all 40 hours are billable, you'll consistently earn less than planned.

Never revisiting your rate. Rates set a year or two ago rarely still reflect your current skill level, demand, or costs. Revisiting your rate every 6-12 months, even just to confirm it's still right, keeps pricing from quietly falling behind.

Underpricing to win the first project with a client. A lower rate at the start of a relationship is far harder to raise later than a fair rate from day one. Clients anchor to whatever number they first agreed to.

Putting it into practice

The most reliable path is usually: calculate your baseline rate from real numbers, decide hourly or project pricing based on the work itself, and revisit the number periodically rather than setting it once and forgetting it.

Pricing well isn't about finding a clever trick, it's about doing the arithmetic honestly and having the confidence to hold the number once it's set.
